ProblemBackgroundMethodNext

Supervised unlearning fine-tunes in two directions at once

\(\theta \leftarrow \theta + \eta\, \nabla_{\theta}\, \mathcal{L}(\theta; D_f)\)

Gradient ascent on the forget set[1]: step up the loss on the dog images, so the model stops answering “dog” for them.

\(\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\, \nabla_{\theta}\, \mathcal{L}(\theta; D_r)\)

Gradient descent on the retain set: keep the loss low on everything else, so cat, bird and plane stay correct.

Together, for a few epochs: \(\displaystyle \min_{\theta}\; \mathcal{L}(\theta; D_r) - \lambda\, \mathcal{L}(\theta; D_f)\) [2]

It needs the forget data, its labels and many gradient steps.

[1] Jang et al., Knowledge Unlearning for Mitigating Privacy Risks in Language Models, ACL 2023 (gradient ascent).
[2] Kurmanji et al., Towards Unbounded Machine Unlearning, NeurIPS 2023 (NegGrad+, ascent on \(D_f\) with descent on \(D_r\)).

Problem · how supervised models unlearn

The common baseline. Split the training data into the forget set \(D_f\) and the retain set \(D_r\). Gradient ascent on \(D_f\) alone is often called NegGrad; adding gradient descent on \(D_r\) is NegGrad+ (both are baselines in, e.g., Kurmanji et al., NeurIPS 2023, which also proposes SCRUB). Other methods (fine-tuning on \(D_r\) only, random relabelling of \(D_f\), influence-based updates) follow the same pattern: a few gradient epochs instead of retraining from scratch.

References. [1] J. Jang, D. Yoon, S. Yang, S. Cha, M. Lee, L. Logeswaran, M. Seo. Knowledge Unlearning for Mitigating Privacy Risks in Language Models. ACL 2023, pp. 14389–14408 (arXiv:2210.01504): gradient ascent on the sequences to forget. [2] M. Kurmanji, P. Triantafillou, J. Hayes, E. Triantafillou. Towards Unbounded Machine Unlearning. NeurIPS 2023 (arXiv:2302.09880): SCRUB, with NegGrad and NegGrad+ (ascent on the forget set combined with descent on the retain set) as baselines.

The gold standard is a model retrained from scratch on \(D_r\); unlearning tries to match it at a fraction of the cost.

Schematic. The loss curves and the probabilities are made up to show the shape of the effect, not measured. The probabilities are the earlier bar-chart scores divided by their total, so each chart sums to 1.

ProblemBackgroundMethodNext

Unlearning in RL removes a behaviour, not just data points

This work is policy unlearning: remove one skill from a vector-symbolic policy by editing its weights, not by retraining.

Problem · machine unlearning and policy unlearning

Machine unlearning usually means erasing the influence of specific training data, such as a user's private photos or copyrighted text, from a classifier or a language model. Policy unlearning targets an RL agent's policy π, its mapping from states to actions: the agent forgets a learned behaviour, trajectory, environment or habit, and keeps its overall competence.

Where it is used. Removing unsafe shortcuts or failure modes from robot and vision-language-action policies; erasing behaviour learned from sensitive trajectories or proprietary environments; and, for language models fine-tuned with RL (PPO, GRPO), on-policy unlearning that suppresses forbidden generation paths.

Not covered here: "policy unlearning" is sometimes also used for models that enforce access-control or governance policies and must forget a revoked rule. This deck uses only the RL meaning.

The drawings are schematic. Left: in a classifier each training sample is separate, so the dog samples can be forgotten (slide 3 shows how) while the others stay as they are. Right: forgetting "pick up" also changes every later state of the trajectory, while lava avoidance, which acts at every step, is kept.

ProblemBackgroundMethodNext

The agent writes what it sees as one long vector H

\(H = \sum_{k} s(k) \odot p(k)\)

s(k): the vector of the object at position k  ·  p(k): the vector of position k

Every object and position is a FHRR vector.

Background · encoding a state

Source. Draft slide 3: \(H = \sum_k s(k) \odot p(k)\), where s(k) is the object encoding and p(k) its positional encoding.

Complex FHRR, as in the paper. Each object vector has entries \(e^{i\psi}\) with \(\psi\) drawn uniformly from \([-\pi, \pi)\). Binding multiplies entry by entry, so angles add; \(\overline{x} \odot x = \mathbf{1}\) because \(e^{-i\psi} e^{i\psi} = 1\). Position vectors are \([p_\ell]_j = e^{i(2\pi m_\ell j / D + \mu_j)}\): rows of the DFT times one shared random phase mask \(\mu\), so different positions are exactly orthogonal and \(p \cdot \overline{p} = D\).

The vectors here are schematic: 8 entries, with angles in whole degrees, shown rounded to two decimals; the positions use DFT rows 0, 1 and 2 (steps of 0°, 45° and 90°). Real hypervectors have about a thousand entries.

ProblemBackgroundMethodNext

Training adds a scaled copy of H to the chosen action's weights

\(W_a \leftarrow W_a + \alpha\, H\)

a: the action the agent took  ·  H: the state it was in

\(\alpha = \dfrac{\eta\, \hat{A}\, (1 - \pi_a)}{\sqrt{D}}\)

\(\eta\): learning rate  ·  \(\hat{A}\): advantage, how much better the outcome was than expected  ·  \(\pi_a\): the probability the policy gave a

Background · training

From the HD-PPO code (src/hdppo/policies.py, SoftmaxLinearPolicy): the logits are \(z_k = H \cdot W_k / (\sqrt{D}\,\tau)\), and the gradient of the clipped PPO surrogate with respect to \(W_k\) is \(r\,\hat{A}\,(\mathbb{1}[a = k] - \pi_k)\, H / (\sqrt{D}\,\tau)\), set to zero where the clip is active. Every gradient is therefore a multiple of H. The slide shows the chosen action with ratio \(r \approx 1\) and \(\tau = 1\); every other action k gets \(-\eta\,\hat{A}\,\pi_k\, H / \sqrt{D}\), a small push the other way. The entropy bonus is also a multiple of H.

Complex FHRR. With \(\operatorname{sim} = \operatorname{Re}(W \cdot \overline{H}) / \sqrt{D}\), the gradient with respect to W is again \(H / \sqrt{D}\), so the update has the same form.

Caveats. The code uses Adam, which rescales each entry, so the step is H with per-entry scaling rather than an exact multiple (exact with plain SGD). The v2 code uses real cos-RFF hypervectors; the slides use complex FHRR as in the paper.

The toy. The α values are chosen so the sum is exactly the toy \(W_{\text{pickup}}\) of the next slides: +0.06, +0.02, −0.06 on key ⊙ ahead, left, right, and +0.03, −0.02 on key ⊙ p4, p5 (other learned information). Each H here is a view with only the key in it. Real views hold many objects, so each update also adds pairs such as wall ⊙ ahead: the part that unbinding turns into noise (slide 9).

ProblemBackgroundMethodNext

The agent takes the action whose weights best match H

\(\mathcal{A} = \{A_1, A_2, \ldots, A_n\}\)

one learned complex weight vector for each of the n actions

\(\operatorname{sim}(A_i, H) = \dfrac{\operatorname{Re}\big(A_i \cdot \overline{H}\big)}{\sqrt{D}}\)

multiply each entry by the conjugate of H's entry, add up, keep the real part; D is the vector length

\(\displaystyle a^{*} = \operatorname*{arg\,max}_{i}\; \operatorname{sim}(A_i, H)\)

the best action is the one with the largest similarity

Background · choosing an action

Source. Draft slides 3 and 4: \(\mathcal{A} = \{A_1, \ldots, A_n\}\), \(\operatorname{sim}(A_i, H) = A_i \cdot H / \sqrt{D}\), \(a^* = \arg\max_i \operatorname{sim}\). With complex vectors the dot product takes the conjugate of H and keeps the real part. Draft slide 4 asked for an animation of the dot product; this is it.

The numbers use the toy vectors in the notes of slide 10. The real parts of the products add to 0.300, and 0.300 / √5 = 0.134, the same value as the draft's example.

The bar chart in step 2 is schematic: there are no weights for the other actions, so their bars only show the argmax.

ProblemBackgroundMethodNext

Unbinding pulls one object's positions out of the weights

A trained weight vector is a sum of many learned concepts, each a bound pair:

\(\displaystyle W_{\text{pickup}} = \begin{cases} \text{Key} \odot \text{Ahead} \\ \text{Key} \odot \text{Left} \\ \text{Wall} \odot \text{Ahead} \\ \text{other learned information} \end{cases}\)

\(U = W_{\text{pickup}} \odot \overline{\text{Key}}\)

unbind: multiply every pair by the conjugate of Key

Because \(\overline{\text{Key}} \odot \text{Key} = \mathbf{1}\), the pairs that contain Key give their position back: Ahead and Left are recovered.

Pairs without Key, such as \(\text{Wall} \odot \text{Ahead}\), do not cancel. They turn into noise: a new random vector, nearly orthogonal to every position.

Background · how unbinding works

Why the key cancels. In complex FHRR every entry of Key is a unit phasor \(e^{i\psi}\), and its conjugate is \(e^{-i\psi}\). Their product is \(e^{0} = 1\) in every entry, so \(\overline{\text{Key}} \odot \text{Key} \odot \text{Ahead} = \text{Ahead}\) exactly.

Why the rest is noise. \(\overline{\text{Key}} \odot \text{Wall} \odot \text{Ahead}\) has angles \(\psi_{\text{wall}} - \psi_{\text{key}} + \psi_{\text{ahead}}\): another random phasor vector. With about a thousand entries its similarity with any position is close to zero (on the order of \(1/\sqrt{D}\)), so comparing U with the positions (next slides) reads off Ahead and Left and ignores it. In the D = 5 toy the leftover sits on positions the edit never looks at, so it is exactly orthogonal by construction.

The brace lists what \(W_{\text{pickup}}\) holds; the weights are the sum (bundle) of those pairs, each with its own learned weight.

ProblemBackgroundMethodNext

Step 1, unbind: cancel the key out of \(W_{\text{pickup}}\)

\(U = W_{\text{pickup}} \odot \overline{\text{key}}\)

Method · step 1, unbind

Source. Draft slide 5: U = \(W_{\text{pickup}}\) ⊙ key and the table p1 (ahead) +0.06, p2 (left) +0.02, p3 (right) −0.06. With complex vectors the key is undone by its conjugate.

The toy vectors (D = 5, complex FHRR, angles in degrees): key = [40, 160, −80, 100, −20]; phase mask μ = [20, −50, 110, 70, −130]; \(p_m\) has angles \(72 m j + \mu_j\) for DFT row m = 0, 1, 2 (ahead, left, right); \(W_{\text{pickup}} = \text{key} \odot (0.06\,p_1 + 0.02\,p_2 - 0.06\,p_3 + 0.03\,p_4 - 0.02\,p_5)\), where \(p_4, p_5\) are DFT rows 3 and 4. Then \(U \cdot \overline{p_j} / 5\) = +0.06, +0.02, −0.06 exactly.

About the toy. With D = 5 there is room for only five orthogonal directions, so "everything else pickup learned" has to sit on DFT rows 3 and 4. With about a thousand entries there is room for the rest of what the action learned.

ProblemBackgroundMethodNext

Step 2, clean up: rebuild U from the known positions

\(M = 0.06\,p_1 + 0.02\,p_2 - 0.06\,p_3\)

Each position vector is weighted by its similarity from step 1, and the weighted vectors are added.

Why not keep U? U also carries everything else pickup learned, mixed up by the unbinding. M keeps only the part that lies along known positions.

Method · step 2, clean up

Source. Draft slide 5, \(M = 0.06\,p_1 + 0.02\,p_2 - 0.06\,p_3\), and its speaker note: rebuild the key's positional information from the known position vectors instead of keeping the noisy unbound vector. The toy reproduces it exactly.

Exact projection. Because the positions are exactly orthogonal, M is the exact projection of U onto them; U − M = 0.03 p4 − 0.02 p5 is what it leaves out. (The earlier ±1 toy had p3 = −p1, so M counted the "ahead" part twice.)

ProblemBackgroundMethodNext

Step 3, rebind and subtract the key's part

\(\text{key} \odot M\)

Binding M with the key again puts “key at those positions” back: this is the key's contribution to \(W_{\text{pickup}}\).

\(W'_{\text{pickup}} = W_{\text{pickup}} - \text{key} \odot M\)

Subtract it, entry by entry.

\(W'_{\text{pickup}}\) keeps what pickup learned about everything else. Only the part that answered “key at a position” is gone.

Method · step 3, rebind and subtract

Source. Draft slide 6: original \(W_{\text{pickup}}\), the key contribution key ⊙ M, and the weights after subtraction. The formulas are the draft's; the entries are complex, so each cell shows the complex number a + bj.

Why it works. \(W' = \text{key} \odot (U - M)\), and U − M is orthogonal to every known \(p_j\). Multiplying by a unit-phasor key keeps dot products, so W′ is orthogonal to key ⊙ \(p_j\) for every known position.

ProblemBackgroundMethodNext

The edit is three vector operations on the trained weights

No retraining. The edit needs no environment steps and no gradient updates, only entry-by-entry products and sums of complex vectors of length D.

Method · the recipe

Summary of slides 10 to 12, written for any action a and any object vector s. For the example, a = pickup and s = key.

The clean-up formula writes the draft's M in general form: the coefficient of \(p_j\) is \(U \cdot \overline{p_j} / (p_j \cdot \overline{p_j})\). For complex FHRR vectors \(p_j \cdot \overline{p_j} = D\), so it is \(U \cdot \overline{p_j} / D\), the draft's formula with the conjugate.
